## 1.1 Proving equalities

<Book file={CH1} section="proving-equalities" parts={["example"]} />

<CourseNote kind="guide" title="Where did the first line come from?">
The first line, $(a+b)^2 = (a-b)^2 + 4ab$, isn't part of the problem. It's the first step of the solution. The author found it with scratch work that a finished proof doesn't show. (Day 2, §1.3, is all about doing that scratch work.) Here's how you could find it yourself.

**Ask what you want and what you know.** You want $(a+b)^2$. You know $a - b$ and $ab$. So try to rewrite $(a+b)^2$ using only $a - b$ and $ab$.

**Expand both squares and compare them.**

$$
(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2
$$

$$
(a-b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2
$$

They're the same except for the middle term. Getting from $-2ab$ to $+2ab$ means adding $4ab$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(a-b)^2 + 4ab &= (a^2 - 2ab + b^2) + 4ab \\
  &= a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \\
  &= (a+b)^2
\end{aligned}
$$

That's the first line. Now both facts appear in it, so the next line can put in $4$ for $a - b$ and $1$ for $ab$.

<details>
<summary>Why not just solve for a and b?</summary>

You can, but it takes many more steps. From $a - b = 4$ you get $a = b + 4$. Put that into $ab = 1$: $(b + 4)b = 1$, so $b^2 + 4b - 1 = 0$. The quadratic formula gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
b &= \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{4^2 - 4\cdot 1\cdot(-1)}}{2} \\
  &= \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 + 4}}{2} \\
  &= \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{20}}{2} \\
  &= \frac{-4 \pm 2\sqrt{5}}{2} \\
  &= -2 \pm \sqrt{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

using $\sqrt{20} = \sqrt{4 \cdot 5} = 2\sqrt{5}$. Then $a = b + 4 = 2 \pm \sqrt{5}$. With the plus signs, $a + b = (2 + \sqrt{5}) + (-2 + \sqrt{5}) = 2\sqrt{5}$, and $(2\sqrt{5})^2 = 4 \cdot 5 = 20$. With the minus signs, $a + b = -2\sqrt{5}$, and $(-2\sqrt{5})^2 = 20$ too. Same answer, much more work.

There's a surprise hiding here: $-2 \pm \sqrt{5}$ isn't a rational number, so no rational $a$ and $b$ satisfy both facts, even though the problem says $a$ and $b$ are rational. The proof is still correct. It shows that *if* $a - b = 4$ and $ab = 1$, then $(a+b)^2 = 20$, and it never needs to know what $a$ and $b$ are.

</details>
</CourseNote>

<CourseNote kind="algebra" title="A picture of (a + b)² = (a − b)² + 4ab">
Both sides come to $a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, so they're equal **for every** $a$ and $b$. That's what makes this step "purely algebraic": it doesn't depend on the facts $a - b = 4$ or $ab = 1$ at all.
